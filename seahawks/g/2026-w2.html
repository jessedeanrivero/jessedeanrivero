Nine straight. Now prove it again.

Seattle has won nine straight against Arizona, but the Cardinals opened the Mike LaFleur era with a 26-14 road win over the Chargers. Arizona controlled the ball for 37:31, gained 393 yards, ran 72 plays and did not turn it over. Seattle comes in 1-0 with its defense still deciding games and Drew Lock making the start for the injured Sam Darnold.

  • Seattle ran only 48 offensive plays in Week 1. Arizona ran 72. Possession and early-down efficiency are the first things to watch.
  • Jacoby Brissett went 27-of-37 for 277 yards with no interceptions. Trey McBride caught nine passes for 95 yards and a touchdown.
  • Lock went 16-of-22 for 187 yards, one touchdown and no interceptions after Darnold left the opener.

The read

Arizona earned attention in Week 1. The Cardinals did not steal the game with one play. They held a 21-snap advantage, gained 125 more yards than the Chargers and played turnover-free football.

Seattle's matchup advantage still starts with the defensive front. Arizona averaged under four yards per running-back carry, while Seattle opened 2026 by crushing New England's run game. If the Seahawks stop the run without crowding the box, Macdonald gets more coverage answers for McBride.

The variable is Lock. Seattle needs longer possessions than it had in the opener. If Lock protects the ball and Seattle gets JSN involved early, the defense should not need three fourth-quarter interceptions to finish the game.

Football IQThe concepts behind this week's sheet

EPA per play

Expected Points Added measures how much each snap changes a team's expected points. It gives more credit to plays that matter in the down-and-distance situation instead of treating every yard equally.

This week: the 2025 baseline still matters more than one Week 1 sample, which is why the model does not treat Arizona as a finished top-five unit after one game.

Simulated pressure

The defense presents more potential rushers than it sends, then drops one expected rusher and attacks from somewhere else. The goal is to make the protection identify the wrong threats while still keeping bodies in coverage.

This week: Brissett played on time against Los Angeles. Seattle needs the disguise to change his first read before the rush arrives.

Possession versus play count

Possession time tells you how long an offense owned the ball. Play count tells you how many chances it created.

This week: Arizona ran 72 plays in Week 1. Seattle ran 48. A repeat gives Arizona 24 more chances to create a mistake or explosive play.

Light box

A light box means the defense keeps fewer defenders close to the line. If the front still stops the run, the defense keeps more bodies for coverage and disguise.

This week: Seattle's front handling Allgeier and Love without safety help frees the secondary to focus on McBride.

Play action

The quarterback shows a handoff before pulling the ball back to throw. It works when linebackers or safeties react to the run and leave space behind them.

This week: early play action gives Lock defined throws without asking him to live in obvious dropback situations.
