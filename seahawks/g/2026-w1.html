The rematch

  • New England's offense finished first in EPA per play and first in passing EPA. Then it lost its top target (Diggs) and added A.J. Brown and Romeo Doubs.
  • Seattle's biggest 2025 weakness was giveaways, 31st in the league. It had zero in the Super Bowl. That's the whole game.

The conclusion

The model has Seattle by 3.2, and for once the model and I agree. It's worth knowing why it changed its mind: EPA per play alone said coin flip, because New England's offense was so good last year that it cancels out Seattle's defense on paper. Elo disagrees, because Elo counts January. Seattle enters this season 141 rating points ahead of New England almost entirely because of what happened in Santa Clara, and playoff wins are the thing efficiency numbers throw away.

Here's what neither can see. Defenses carry over year to year better than offenses do, and Seattle's defense returns its core: Williams, Lawrence, Nwosu, Murphy, Emmanwori, Ernest Jones. New England's offense turned over its receiver room and now has to learn A.J. Brown and Romeo Doubs on the fly against the unit that held it to 13 points seven months ago. Maye's 8.7% sack rate was the crack in that offense all year, and Macdonald's disguise got to him six times in the Super Bowl. Add that Macdonald is year three into a scheme that finished 2nd and 1st in his previous two second years, and this is a bad week to be a new receiver corps.

On the other side, Seattle's offense has its own problems. The run game was 21st before Walker left, and Charbonnet may not play. Darnold's 14 interceptions are the reason this isn't a comfortable pick. If he gives it away twice, New England wins anyway.

I'll stay at 23-20 rather than chase the model up to 25. The market has this at 44.5 total and I think that's right: two top-five scoring defenses, offenses with new parts, a Wednesday night crowd that's going to be deafening. Low twenties both sides, Seattle by a field goal, and the unit that didn't change decides it.

How the model worksInputs, backtest, Elo, and quarterback math

Four inputs. Team ratings: EPA per play on offense and allowed on defense, blending the current season with last season, last season fading as games get played. Elo: a running rating that moves with margin of victory and reverts partway each offseason, including playoff results. Quarterback: opponent-adjusted EPA per dropback for tonight's starter, measured against the team's own recent baseline so nobody gets counted twice. Home field. The margin is a straight line through those; the total is a straight line through both offenses and defenses; win probability is how often a margin that size holds up given the historical error. Fit on 1,855 regular season games, 2019 to 2025.

Not in the model: injuries other than at quarterback, offseason roster changes, weather, rest and travel, coaching, anything that happened after the last snap of the stat season. Read the conclusion for those.

Translation: it picks the winner about 65% of the time and misses the margin by 10 points on average. The betting market picks 66%, so this is within about a point and a half of Vegas. In weeks 1 through 3 it actually edges the market, 64% to 63%, because early lines are guessing from last year too. Expect it to be wrong about one week in three, and don't bet on it.

Elo, explained

Elo is a running rating that moves after every game by how much you won or lost by and how surprising the result was. Beat a much better team by a lot and you take a big chunk of their rating. It reverts partway toward average each offseason, because rosters change.

It catches things EPA per play does not. EPA measures how efficiently you moved the ball; Elo measures whether you actually won, including in January. Seattle enters this season +141 Elo points ahead of New England largely because of what happened in February, and that is worth +3.7 points tonight. Playoff games count.

Team EPA ratings already contain whatever the quarterback did, so you cannot just add "good QB" on top or you count him twice. Instead the model rates tonight's starter against the team's own recent quarterback baseline. Same guy as usual, no adjustment. Backup in for an injured starter, big adjustment. A rating travels with the quarterback when he changes teams, which is the entire point.

Both teams are starting the same quarterback they have been starting, so this adjustment is zero tonight. That is the correct answer, not a broken feature. D.Maye rates +0.086 EPA per dropback above S.Darnold, and that gap is already inside New England's team rating. Adding it again would count him twice.

The coefficient is +20.2 points per unit of EPA per dropback. A backup who is 0.10 worse than the starter costs his team about 2.0 points.

The same regression rates defenses. Hardest to throw on: CLE (-0.16), PHI (-0.16), MIN (-0.15). Easiest: WAS (+0.06), TEN (+0.05), ARI (+0.03). Those numbers are what gets divided out of each quarterback.

Football IQThe concepts used above, explained once

EPA per play

Expected Points Added. Every down, distance, and field position has an expected point value. EPA is how much a play moved that number. Positive is good for the offense. Average it over a season and you get the single best measure of how good a unit actually is, because it credits a 4-yard gain on 3rd and 3 more than a 4-yard gain on 3rd and 10.

Tonight: Seattle's defense allowed -0.111 EPA per play last year. Every snap, the opponent's expected points went down by about a tenth of a point. Over 1,000 plays that's the difference between the best defense in the league and an average one.

Success rate

The share of plays that gained enough to stay on schedule: roughly 40% of the distance on first down, 60% on second, 100% on third and fourth. It measures consistency where EPA measures impact. A team can have great EPA from a few explosive plays and a bad success rate, which usually means it's boom or bust.

Tonight: New England's passing game was first in both. Seattle's run game was 21st in EPA and 23rd in success rate, which is why the pass has to carry the offense.

Pass rate over expected

Given the down, distance, score, and clock, how often would an average team throw here? PROE is how much more or less a team actually throws than that. It tells you the play caller's personality, not just his situation.

Tonight: Seattle was -4.1 on neutral early downs last year, meaning it ran more than the situation called for. New England was +3.9. Same record, opposite philosophies.

Simulated pressure

The defense shows five or six potential rushers before the snap, then sends four, but not the four you expected. A lineman drops into coverage and a linebacker or safety comes instead. The quarterback's protection was set for the wrong people. You get blitz-like confusion with zone-coverage numbers behind it.

Tonight: This is the Macdonald signature. Seattle rushed fewer players than almost anyone and still finished first in defensive EPA. The pressure comes from the offense blocking the wrong guy.

Average depth of target

aDOT. How far past the line of scrimmage the ball travels on an average throw. High aDOT means an aggressive, downfield passing game. Low aDOT means quick game and checkdowns. Defenses can force a low aDOT by taking away the deep ball and making the quarterback settle.

Tonight: Maye's 9.2 was the second highest in the league. Quarterbacks facing Seattle averaged 6.6, the lowest. Tonight's first deep ball tells you which one bends.

Box count

The number of defenders lined up within roughly five yards of the line of scrimmage before a run. Seven is standard. Eight or more is a loaded box: the defense is daring you to throw. The offense's answer to a loaded box is play action or a quick throw to the vacated area.

Tonight: New England saw eight-man boxes on 21.6% of its runs last year, second most in the league, because defenses would rather stop Henderson than cover Maye's receivers with an extra man. It still worked because Maye punished it.
